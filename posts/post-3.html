A very small guide to reading train timetables

August 24, 2026 · 4 min read · Travel

Everyone reads the departure column. It is the big one, on the left, in the font that the designer clearly enjoyed. It is also the least useful column on the board.

Read right to left

Start at the far right, where the tiny type lives, and work backwards. That is where the footnotes are: the letters and daggers and small circles that tell you this service does not run on Sundays, or runs only during school terms, or terminates three stops before the one you wanted and expects you to have known that.

A timetable is a legal document that has been laid out by someone who was told to make it look friendly. The friendliness is in the left column. The truth is in the right one.

The four symbols that actually matter

Most networks use some version of the same four. A letter in a circle means the service is seasonal or term-time. A dagger means it does not run on public holidays, which are not the same as Sundays and are listed on a different page. A hollow square means it requires a reservation. And a superscript number is a footnote that will, without exception, be at the bottom of the following page.

Arrival times are promises, departure times are contracts

The departure time is when the train is not allowed to leave before. The arrival time is when it hopes to be there. These are different kinds of number and printing them in the same typeface is, in my view, a small daily act of dishonesty.

If you have a connection of less than the printed minimum interchange time, you do not have a connection. You have a plan.

The minimum interchange time is printed once, usually in the front matter, usually as a table of station codes. Nobody reads the front matter. It is the most valuable page in the book.

The Sunday problem

Sunday services are often a separate timetable entirely, not a subset of the weekday one, and they frequently use different platforms, different stopping patterns, and occasionally a bus. The bus will be listed in the same column as the trains, in the same type, with a small letter beside it that you will find out about at the station.

My rule now is simple: if I am travelling on a Sunday, I look the journey up twice, on two different days, and if the two answers differ I assume the worse one is right. It has not failed me yet, which is not the same as it being a good rule.
