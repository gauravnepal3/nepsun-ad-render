Why my coffee grinder is louder than my neighbour's dog

September 8, 2026 · 6 min read · Notes

The grinder arrived in a box that was mostly air. I had read four reviews, two of which appeared to be the same review, and I had convinced myself that the difference between a good cup and the cup I had been drinking for six years was sitting in that box, waiting.

It was not. What was in the box was a motor with opinions.

The first morning I ran it, my partner came into the kitchen holding both ears and asked, without any particular urgency, whether something had fallen off the building. The dog next door — a beagle with a documented history of responding to sirens, bin lorries, and the letterbox — did not react at all. I took this personally.

Measuring the thing

I borrowed a sound meter app, which is roughly as reliable as asking a stranger to guess your weight, and stood in three places: at the counter, in the hallway, and outside the back door with the window open. The numbers were 84, 71, and 63. The beagle, for comparison, peaked at 78 when the postman arrived at 9:40.

The grinder was not louder than the dog. It was louder than the dog for longer, which turns out to be the part that matters.

Eighteen seconds of grinding, every morning, at a frequency that sits exactly where human hearing is most sensitive. The dog barks in bursts. The grinder is a sustained argument.

What actually helped

Three things, in descending order of effect. First, a rubber mat under the base, which killed the resonance through the countertop and took maybe six decibels off the low end. Second, grinding into a metal tin rather than the plastic hopper, which sounds like it should not matter and matters enormously. Third — and this is the one nobody mentions — grinding slightly coarser, because the motor stops straining.

The chart I made at six in the morning suggested the mat was worth twelve decibels. It was not. I had left the app running while the kettle boiled.

The part where I admit it

The coffee is better. It is meaningfully, obviously better, and I would not go back. But the thing I had told myself — that the grinder was the last piece of a puzzle — was wrong in the way that most equipment stories are wrong. The beans were the puzzle. I had been buying them from a supermarket shelf where they had been sitting since a date I would rather not think about.

I switched to a roaster two suburbs over who prints the roast date on the bag in pen. That did more than the grinder. It also cost about a fortieth as much, which is the sort of fact you discover in the correct order roughly never.

Notes for anyone in the same box

Burr size matters less than burr alignment, and alignment is the thing nobody can check in a shop. Conical burrs are quieter than flat ones at the same wattage, mostly because they spin slower. If you live in a flat with neighbours below you, the mat is not optional, and neither is not grinding before seven.

And if you are reading this at six in the morning, deciding whether to press the button: the beagle is awake anyway. It has been awake since five. It is only polite to join in.
